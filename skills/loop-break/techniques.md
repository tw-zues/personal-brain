## The Self-Reinforcing Loop Architecture

Based on the integrated evidence from 27 papers, thought loops persist through a
multi-level self-reinforcing system:

**Cognitive-affective level (Nolen-Hoeksema, 1991; 2008):**
Negative mood → activates negative memories via associative networks → person
focuses on emotional state (rumination) → uses mood as information for self-evaluation
→ generates more negative cognitions → deepens negative mood → repeat. Ruminators
generated only 2.7 solutions to problems vs. 6.3 after distraction.

**Processing-mode level (Watkins, 2005; 2008):**
Rumination operates in abstract evaluative mode ("Why? What does this mean?").
Abstract processing impairs problem-solving, produces broader generalizations from
single events, and interrupts performance. The loop persists not because the person
is "thinking too much" but because they're thinking at the wrong *level of abstraction*.

**Metacognitive level (Wells, 1995; 2008):**
Positive metacognitive beliefs ("I need to worry to stay prepared") MOTIVATE the
loop. Negative metacognitive beliefs ("My worrying is uncontrollable") create
meta-worry — worry about the worry. Unhelpful coping (suppression, avoidance,
reassurance-seeking) maintains the whole cycle. The CAS is the engine.

**Emotion-regulation level (Gross, 1998; 2003):**
Suppression (trying not to feel it) leaves experience intact, adds physiological
stress, drains cognitive resources, creates inauthenticity. The failed regulation
strategy becomes a NEW object of rumination.

**Neurocognitive level (Dreisbach & Goschke, 2004):**
Thought loops = cognitive perseveration — inability to disengage from a stuck set.
Negative affect does NOT promote the flexibility needed to switch. The loop is
self-locking: the negative emotion it generates cannot provide the neurochemical
conditions (dopaminergic gating) for escape.

**Neural-predictive level (Sokolov / Kirvelis):**
The brain only allocates attention on prediction error (mismatch). Repetitive
thoughts MATCH the internal model → no orienting reflex → no reorientation.
The loop runs on autopilot because the brain treats it as "expected."

**Ironic process level (Wegner, 1994):**
Suppression activates a dual-process system: an effortful operator (searches for
distractors) and an automatic monitor (scans for the suppressed thought). Under
cognitive load — always present during loops — the operator degrades while the
monitor persists, flooding consciousness with the suppressed content. The attempt
to escape the loop IS the loop's fuel. Self-loading ironic cycles: intrusion →
distress → increased load → degraded operator → more intrusions.

**Experiential avoidance level (Cribb et al., 2006):**
Rumination functions as avoidance — abstract "why" processing prevents activation
of concrete emotional/somatic experience. People ruminate BECAUSE it hurts less
than feeling. The loop is a shield, not just a trap.

**Cognitive-exhaustion level (Starr & Storey, 2025):**
Being stuck = running out of "things to try." The person cycles through the same
failed approaches based on unexamined assumptions. Confusion is fatiguing,
producing "blindness effects" — literally not seeing what's in front of you.

## The Optimal Intervention Sequence

Based on the integrated evidence:

### Step 1: Create cognitive flexibility (~1-2 sentences)
**Mechanism:** Brief positive affect reduces perseveration ~80% via dopaminergic
gating (Dreisbach & Goschke, 2004). Alternatively, introduce mismatch/novelty to
trigger the orienting reflex (Sokolov).
**In practice:** Warmth, humor, genuine appreciation, or a surprising observation.
**Constraint:** The window comes with increased distractibility — Step 2 must
follow immediately to capture it.

### Step 2: Execute ONE interrupt technique
Match to loop type. See SKILL.md Phase 2 for the full toolkit.

### Step 3: Redirect to concrete action or values
**Mechanism:** Defusion/decentering alone is insufficient. The cleared path must
lead somewhere. Values-consistent action (Hayes, 2006) or concrete next steps
(Watkins, 2005) prevent re-engagement with loop content.

### Step 4: Register the break for neuroplasticity
**Mechanism:** Each successful decentered experience creates a stored memory
(Teasdale, 2002) accessible during future episodes. Name the shift explicitly.

## Interrupt Templates

### The Specificity Interrupt (Watkins)
```
"You said [abstract statement]. Get specific with me — when was the last time
that actually happened? Walk me through exactly what occurred."
```
**Mechanism:** Forces shift from abstract evaluative processing to concrete
experiential processing. At equivalent mood levels, this improves problem-solving
effectiveness from 2.65→3.55 (Watkins & Moulds, 2005).

### The Process-Naming Interrupt (Wells)
```
"Notice what you're doing right now: you're [running a simulation / replaying
a scene / scanning for threats / building a case for hopelessness]. That's a
process, not a conclusion. What has this round of thinking produced that the
last five rounds didn't?"
```
**Mechanism:** Shifts from object mode (fused with content) to metacognitive
mode (observing the process). Targets the CAS directly.

### The Defusion Interrupt (Hayes / Masuda)
```
"You said '[their statement]' like it's a fact. Try this: 'I'm having the
thought that [their statement].' Feel the difference? One is a thought. The
other is reality. They're not the same thing."
```
**Mechanism:** Changes the CONTEXT in which the thought occurs, not its content.
Reduces believability — which is the variable that mediates outcomes, not
frequency (Hayes, 2006).

### The Word Repetition Protocol (Masuda, 2004)
Full protocol for persistent identity-level fusion:
1. Demonstrate with "milk" — say it once (associations flood in: white, cold,
   creamy). Then repeat it aloud fast for 30 seconds. Associations dissolve.
   The word becomes sound.
2. Apply to the target thought: reduce it to a single core word ("worthless,"
   "failure," "fraud," "can't"). Repeat fast for 30 seconds.
3. Re-rate: "What happened to the word?"
**Evidence:** Superior to both distraction and thought control across all
participants on every trial. Effect was entirely due to the experiential
exercise, not the rationale (rationale alone was insufficient).

### The Frame-Break Interrupt (Second-Order Change)
```
"You're trying to solve [X] by doing more of [Y]. But what if [Y] is why
[X] exists? What would you do if [Y] wasn't an option at all?"
```
**Mechanism:** Identifies first-order change (more of the same within the frame)
and introduces second-order change (changing the frame itself).

### The Meta-Worry Interrupt (Wells, 1995)
```
"You've worried thousands of times in your life. Has it ever made you go
crazy? Has it ever been truly uncontrollable — or does it just feel that way
while it's running? What if the worry itself isn't the problem — but your
belief that you MUST worry is?"
```
**Mechanism:** Targets Type 2 worry directly. Wells (1995) case: targeting ONLY
meta-beliefs reduced worry from 11.8 hours/week to 1.73 hours/week in one session.

### The Attention Redirect (Wells, 1997; 1998)
```
"Your attention has been locked on [threat/problem]. Not because it's the most
important thing — but because your threat-monitoring system flagged it and
won't let go. Give me something in your work right now that's actually going
well that you haven't thought about today."
```
**Mechanism:** Self-focused attention causally maintains loops (Wells, 1997:
reversal design). External attention + exposure is 4.5x more effective than
exposure alone (Wells, 1998: 45-point vs 9.6-point belief reduction).

### The Behavioral Activation Interrupt (Nolen-Hoeksema)
```
"Understanding why won't change this. Doing something different will. What's
one concrete action you can take in the next 2 hours that moves you — even
slightly — out of this position?"
```
**Mechanism:** Ruminative response style impairs both solution generation AND
willingness to implement. Active, engrossing distraction breaks the mood-
cognition cycle, enabling subsequent effective problem-solving.

### The Assumption-Surfacing Interrupt (Starr & Storey, 2025)
```
"Walk me through what you've already tried. Not the problem — the approaches.
... Now: what are you assuming is true that you haven't actually verified?"
```
**Mechanism:** Articulation forcing (rubber ducking) restructures the mental
representation and reveals hidden assumptions. Being stuck = cycling through
the same approaches because of unexamined assumptions about the problem.

### The Suppression-Redirect (Gross, 1998)
```
"Trying not to feel it is making it worse — suppression leaves the feeling
intact and adds tension on top. Instead: what's a genuinely different way to
see this specific situation?"
```
**Mechanism:** Suppression = response-focused (too late, zero effect on experience,
adds physiological stress). Reappraisal = antecedent-focused (alters the entire
emotional trajectory with no physiological cost).

### The Strategic Pause (Kupor & Tormala, 2015)
When delivering a key reframe, strategically interrupt yourself:
```
"The pattern I see is that every solution you've tried is more of the — actually,
quick question: when was the last time you felt genuinely curious about your
work? ... Okay, hold that feeling. Now here's what I notice: every 'solution'
is more of the same strategy that built the ceiling."
```
**Mechanism:** Momentary interruption before key insight creates a curiosity gap
that increases processing depth. The person leans INTO the reframe rather than
deflecting it. Works best with strong arguments, before (not after) the insight,
on people not already deeply engaged.

### The Implementation Intention Lock (Gollwitzer, 1999)
After ANY successful interrupt, lock the exit with an if-then plan:
```
"Now complete this sentence: 'When I notice [earliest cue of the loop],
I will [specific alternative response].'"
```
**Mechanism:** A single if-then plan creates instant automaticity — the cue
becomes hyperaccessible and the response fires without deliberation. This
bypasses Wegner's ironic monitor entirely because it delegates control to the
environment rather than relying on effortful suppression.

**Evidence:** Worked for opiate addicts in withdrawal (80% vs 0%), frontal
lobe patients (effect STRONGER with more impairment), and exercise compliance
(39% → 91%).

**Template examples by domain:**
```
Rumination:    "When I notice the 'why did I...' thought, I will say 'rerun'
               and name what's in front of me right now."
Business:      "When I catch myself checking competitors, I will open my
               client feedback file."
Relationship:  "When I feel the urge to replay the argument, I will text
               [friend] about something unrelated."
Catastrophizing: "When I notice a 'what if' chain starting, I will ask
               'what's the concrete next step?'"
Perfectionism: "When I feel 'it's not ready,' I will ship the smallest
               viable version."
```

**Key constraint:** "Distraction-inhibiting" format ("I will ignore it")
outperforms "effort-mobilizing" format ("I will try harder"). Cold beats hot.
Consistent with Wegner: effort under load backfires.

## Resistance Patterns and Responses

### "Yes, but..." resistance
They acknowledge your point but immediately return to the loop.
```
"You've 'yes-butted' three things now. That's data — not about the situation,
about the loop. What are you protecting by staying in this thinking pattern?"
```
**Mechanism:** Surfaces the avoidance function of rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2008:
rumination builds a case for hopelessness that justifies inaction).

### "You don't understand" resistance
They feel the interrupt dismissed their experience.
```
"You're right that I'm not in it the way you are. But I can see the process
from outside. You're [running simulations / building a case / threat-monitoring].
Am I wrong about that?"
```
**Mechanism:** Validates their experience while maintaining the meta-level frame.
Targets the process, not the content.

### "I just need to figure it out" resistance
Rumination disguised as problem-solving.
```
"How long have you been figuring it out? Has the figuring produced a decision?
Ruminators in Nolen-Hoeksema's studies believed they were problem-solving but
generated half as many solutions as people who distracted first. You're not
solving — you're spinning."
```
**Mechanism:** Directly names the process-outcome gap. The person feels productive
but is not — confronting this gap creates the mismatch needed for reorientation.

### Silent re-loop (they seem to hear you, then spiral back)
```
"We're back in the same loop. I'll be direct: what decision or action are you
avoiding underneath all this analysis? Because the loop is running cover for
something."
```
**Mechanism:** Targets avoidance function directly. The loop provides a reason
to stay still. Naming this removes the cover.

### "I can't stop" (meta-worry)
```
"That feeling of 'I can't stop' is a belief about your worry, not a fact
about your capacity. You've stopped thousands of worry episodes in your
life — you just don't notice the stops because they're unremarkable. Has
this worry ever actually been uncontrollable, or does it just feel that way
while you're in it?"
```
**Mechanism:** Type 2 worry — negative metacognitive belief about controllability
(Wells, 1995). The belief IS the maintaining factor, not the worry itself.

## The Metacognitive Awareness Ladder (Teasdale, 2002)

Teasdale's MACAM identified 5 levels. Lower levels predicted depressive relapse
INDEPENDENT of depression severity. Both CT and MBCT increased metacognitive
awareness — and this change (not belief change) mediated relapse prevention.

**Level 1: Swamped.** Immersed in undifferentiated bad feelings. Global descriptions
("bad," "awful," "crap"). No distance from thoughts.
→ Guide to Level 2: "Can you separate what you're feeling? Is there anger? Fear?
Sadness? Frustration? What's the strongest one?"

**Level 2: Discriminating feelings.** Can identify different emotional strands.
Precision of description matters more than count.
→ Guide to Level 3: "So that's the feeling. Is it the same thing as the situation,
or is it your reaction to the situation?"

**Level 3: Self/situation distinction.** Recognizes internal generation of feelings.
May acknowledge views might change with mood or perspective.
→ Guide to Level 4: "You said 'I'm failing.' Can you notice that as a thought
you're having about yourself, rather than a fact about reality?"

**Level 4: Self/thought distinction.** Can "stand back from the depression."
Reports being able to "see what's going on."
→ Guide to Level 5: "What happens to the thought's power when you look at it
from the outside?"

**Level 5: Persistent decentered awareness.** Metacognitive perspective reached
quickly, clearly, persistently.

**The critical distinction (Teasdale, 2002):**
Metacognitive KNOWLEDGE ("I know thoughts aren't facts") ≠ metacognitive INSIGHT
(actually experiencing thoughts as mental events in real time). Knowledge alone
is insufficient. Only the experiential shift produces lasting change. This is why
the Masuda word-repetition exercise and guided MACAM questions work better than
psychoeducation alone.

## Neuroplasticity Progression Model

### Stage 1: External Interrupt (starting point)
Someone else (or this skill) breaks the loop.
**Marker:** "Oh, I was doing it again."
**Training:** Simply notice after the fact. Each notice creates a new metacognitive
memory (Teasdale, 2002) accessible during future episodes.

### Stage 2: Real-Time Recognition
Catching yourself mid-loop without external help.
**Marker:** "Wait, I'm looping."
**Training:** Label the loop type: "That's catastrophizing" / "That's rumination" /
"That's threat-monitoring." Labeling engages prefrontal cortex and dampens amygdala
response (affect labeling research). Naming the process shifts from object mode
to metacognitive mode (Wells, 2008).

### Stage 3: Rapid Self-Interrupt
Catch + redirect within seconds.
**Marker:** Loop starts → name it → "what's the concrete next step?" → move on.
**Training:** Build a personal interrupt phrase. Something short that becomes your
exit ramp: "What's the move?" / "Zoom in." / "That's a rerun." / "Process, not
content."

### Stage 4: Pattern Immunity
Certain loops stop triggering because the response pathway is rewired.
**Marker:** A situation that used to spiral you barely registers.
**Training:** Notice the ABSENCE of the loop. Your brain needs the positive signal
that the old pathway wasn't activated.

### Cross-Domain Transfer

The most powerful neuroplasticity play: each successful loop-break in one domain
makes you faster at recognizing loops in every domain. The meta-skill (recognizing
the PROCESS of getting stuck) is domain-independent.

- Break a rumination loop about a relationship → faster at spotting analysis
  paralysis in business
- Break a marketing positioning loop → faster at catching comparison spirals
- Break a perfectionism loop → faster at stopping catastrophizing about finances

**After every successful interrupt:** "Where else does this same pattern show up?"

### Long-Term Attention Training (Wells ATT)

For sustained neuroplasticity, the ATT protocol practiced 12 minutes daily when
calm (NOT during anxiety — that makes it a safety behavior):

1. **Selective attention** (~5 min): Focus on one sound, ignore competing sounds
2. **Rapid switching** (~5 min): Shift between sounds at different spatial locations
3. **Divided attention** (~2 min): Expand awareness to absorb all sounds simultaneously

This builds the executive control infrastructure that makes loop-breaking automatic.
Wells (1997) reversal design: ATT reduced anxiety; self-focus reignited it; ATT
re-introduction reduced it again. The causal relationship is established.

## Domain Application Matrix

|--------|-------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Personal rumination** | Past replay, self-blame | Abstract evaluative processing (Watkins) | "What specifically happened? Walk me through the concrete details." |
| **Anxiety/catastrophizing** | Future what-if chains | CAS threat monitoring (Wells) | "That's a simulation, not a prediction. What's the actual decision in front of you?" |
| **Business strategy** | Optimizing the wrong thing | First-order change within wrong frame | "What would you STOP doing if it wouldn't affect revenue for 6 months?" |
| **Marketing** | Can't see own positioning | Cognitive fusion with category | "Tell me about one client who chose you. What did they say?" |
| **Relationship** | Same dynamic, different people | Pattern-level loop, own role invisible | "What's YOUR move at this point? What do you always do here?" |
| **Creative block** | Perfectionism / unachievable criteria | Unachievable stop signals (Fisher & Wells) | "What's your actual criteria for 'done'? Is it achievable or is it a feeling?" |
| **Decision paralysis** | Infinite research, no action | Rumination as avoidance (Nolen-Hoeksema) | "What decision are you avoiding? The research is running cover." |
| **Identity crisis** | "I am [negative]" | Cognitive fusion (Hayes) | Defusion: "You're having the thought that..." |
| **Competitive anxiety** | Threat-monitoring market | CAS externalized (Wells) | "What happens if you stop scanning competitors for one month?" |
| **Being stuck (general)** | Exhausted "things to try" | Assumption-based cycling (Starr & Storey) | "Walk me through what you've tried. What assumption haven't you tested?" |
